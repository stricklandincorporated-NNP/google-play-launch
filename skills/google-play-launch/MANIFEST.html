Google Play Launch Manifest

The full path from nothing to a live Google Play listing — written for someone who has never done this before. Every step says what to click, in what order, and why it exists. Phase 00 is what an agent can do for you unattended; everything after is tagged for how often you'll do it yourself. Every link here was checked before this file shipped.

How to use this

With an agent

In Claude Code, from this project's root:

/google-play-launch

Or in plain words: “get this app ready for Google Play.”

It runs recon, writes GOOGLE-PLAY-PLAN.md, then works through Phase 00 below — ticking off each item as it lands, and copying this file to store-assets/MANIFEST.html so the checklist lives with the app it describes.

Solo

00
Agent run
Agent · unattended

One agent pass covers all the local work — recon, icons, splash screens, config files, screenshots and drafted listing copy.

  • Detects whether this is a native Gradle project, Capacitor, Expo, React Native or Flutter app — or a web app that needs a native shell added. Writes a short plan naming what will change and what needs a human, before touching anything.
    Why

    Every later step branches on the answer. The command that builds a Flutter app is not the one that builds a Capacitor app, and the file an icon lands in differs by stack. Writing the plan first also gives you a chance to say “no, don't do that” before anything on disk changes.

  • One pass over the real source for fetch(, XMLHttpRequest, WebSocket, analytics and ad SDK imports, storage key namespaces, and every permission actually declared. Google's Data safety form is answered from this one file.
    Why

    Answering the Data safety form from memory, days or months after writing the code, is how it ends up disagreeing with what the app actually does — exactly the kind of mismatch that surfaces after launch, when fixing it means a new submission.

    Writing the facts down once also means next release you re-check a file instead of re-remembering an app.

  • A plain-HTML policy naming what is collected, what stays on the device, what leaves it, and how to contact you. Play wants a public URL to it, not a separate document.
    Why

    Play rejects a listing with no privacy policy URL, and will remove an app whose policy URL later 404s. A single file hosted anywhere public — GitHub Pages is free and fine — satisfies it.

    Keeping it as one document is the point: a policy that drifts from what the Data safety form says is exactly what a reviewer catches.

  • Greps the built output, not the config file that claims to exclude things.
    Why

    A build flag that is supposed to strip dev tooling can silently stop working, and the config will keep claiming it is excluded. Auditing the artifact itself is the only check that actually holds — and a debug endpoint or an unlocked paywall in a public build is a security problem, not a cosmetic one.

Everything an agent can do for you before you ever need a Play Console account. All local repo work — recon, generated assets, config edits, and drafted copy. See the google-play-launch skill for the exact procedure and the commands behind each item. Going solo? Skip to Before you begin.

  • Scaffolded if missing, with whichever tool the detected stack calls for. Confirmed to build before anything else proceeds.
    Why

    Google Play does not accept websites, React projects, or zip files — it accepts a signed Android App Bundle, and only an Android/Gradle project can produce one. The “Android project” is the Gradle-shaped wrapper that turns your app into that bundle. Without it there is literally nothing to upload.

  • The package name (applicationId) is asked, never invented — it's a reverse-DNS identifier you can't change after your first upload, and anything still on com.example.* is refused by Play outright. Display name and version numbers are kept consistent across the Gradle config and any framework config that mirrors them.
    Why

    Multi-stack projects store the same three facts in two or three places (build.gradle, plus something like capacitor.config.json or app.json). When they drift, you get a build that installs under one name and reports another — and Play Console rejects the upload with a mismatch error that doesn't say which file is wrong.

  • Rasterized from a source SVG/PNG, then generated into res/mipmap-* (including the adaptive foreground/background layers) plus a 512×512 PNG for the Play listing.
    Why

    Android launchers show the “adaptive” icon — two layers the launcher masks into whatever shape the device uses — while the store listing shows a separate 512×512 upload. Unlike Apple, Google applies the corner rounding itself, so a full-square icon is correct and pre-rounded corners come out visibly wrong. Miss a density and older devices show a blurry upscale of the wrong size.

  • Splash variants land in the drawable resources; on Android 12+ the system splash API crops the mark into a centered circle, so the source art keeps the logo inside the middle third.
    Why

    Android shows the splash for the second or two before your app draws anything. If it's missing or broken, the app opens to a blank flash — which reads as “broken” in review and to every first-time user. The Android-12 circle crop is the gotcha: art that looked fine on Android 11 gets its edges cut off on every modern device.

  • Orientation locked on the main activity where the app is single-orientation; the merged permission list audited and anything the app doesn't genuinely use removed.
    Why

    AndroidManifest.xml is the app's ID card — Android reads it before running a single line of your code. Permissions are the part with consequences: every one you declare shows on your store listing, widens the Data safety form, and some (SMS, call log, background location, all-files access) trigger extra Play declarations and likely rejection. Libraries silently inject their own permissions into the merged manifest, which is why the audit reads the merge, not just your source file.

  • Checked against Google's live requirement page, not a remembered number — the floor rises every August 31. Current floor Target API level requirements
    Why

    Google requires apps to target a recent Android version — roughly “within one year of the latest release” — and enforces it at upload: a bundle targeting below the floor is refused before any human ever sees it. As of August 31, 2026 the floor for new apps is Android 16 (API 36). This is also the single most common failure for someone returning to a project after a year away, which is why it gets its own box.

  • App name, short description, and full description — each checked against Google's field limits (30 / 80 / 4000 characters), not estimated.
    Why

    Play Console refuses over-length fields, and you'll be editing them in a cramped web form. Drafting them in a file first means you paste finished text instead of writing marketing copy inside a browser text box at 11pm. On Play the copy also is your search strategy — there's no separate keyword field, so the description text does double duty.

  • Play-only, required, and no icon generator makes it: a 1024×500 banner composed from the app's mark and name on a brand background.
    Why

    The feature graphic is the wide image at the top of your Play listing, and Play Console won't let you publish without one. It has no iOS equivalent, which is exactly why it gets forgotten until the final form blocks on it. It's also what Google uses if the app is ever featured or shown with a video thumbnail — a lazy solid rectangle is a real cost, not a formality.

  • Automated where the stack allows it — headless Chromium for a web-shell app (Android's WebView is Chromium), the emulator's adb screencap for a native one. At least 2 phone shots, up to 8; JPEG or 24-bit PNG with no alpha channel; each side between 320px and 3840px.
    Why

    Play is looser than Apple about exact pixel sizes but strict about format — a PNG with an alpha channel is silently refused. Automating the set also means you can re-shoot everything in seconds when the UI changes, instead of hand-cropping phone screenshots. Tablet sets (7″ and 10″) only matter if you want Play to present the app as tablet-optimized.

  • Never reported done without actually being run. If a step can't run in this environment (no JDK, no Android SDK), that's stated plainly instead of assumed.
    Why

    Everything above edits native config files. A config edit that looks right and breaks the build is normal, not exotic — and you'd otherwise discover it in Android Studio an hour later with no idea which of a dozen changes caused it.

PREP
Before you begin
Read once · start here if you're new

Nothing to do here — just the things that surprise people on day one. Five minutes of reading that saves two weeks of confusion, because Android's biggest wait is one almost nobody knows about going in.

  • Android Studio runs on all three, and everything in this checklist can be done from any of them. You don't strictly need an Android phone either — the emulator covers most testing — but one real device is strongly recommended, and you'll want it anyway as one of your own test installs.
    Why

    This is the mirror image of iOS, where a Mac is mandatory. Android's build tools are free and cross-platform, which is why the barrier to entry here is money-cheap and calendar-expensive — the waits below, not the hardware, are what gate you.

    The real device matters for two reasons: emulators don't reproduce real performance, cameras, or notification behavior; and when you recruit testers in Phase 06, being able to verify the opt-in flow on an actual phone yourself saves a round-trip through every confused tester.

  • People lose hours to this. Bookmark both now. Your apps play.google.com/console — the Play Console: app records, listings, releases, testers, declarations, money. How to build developer.android.com — Android Studio, SDK docs, signing and bundle documentation.
    Why

    Roughly: developer.android.com is about building the app (tools, code, packaging), and the Play Console is about shipping it (what the public sees, what builds exist, what you get paid). Google's help center for the Console is a third place — Play Console Help — and almost every “I can't find that setting” moment ends there.

    Why

    Each of these is a one-way door people walk through casually. The public-address rule surprises second — it's a consumer-protection requirement, and the only way around it for an individual is a registered business entity (or a mailbox address that is legally yours).

    Google's error messages are written for people who already speak this vocabulary — “Your Android App Bundle is signed with the wrong key” means nothing until you know what an upload key is and that there are two keys in play. Everything here was accurate when written, but the live Play Console screen always wins over any checklist, including this one. When a number here disagrees with what's on your screen, believe your screen.
01
Developer accounts
One-time · per person or company

Everything downstream — every app you ever ship — hangs off this one registration. Get the account type right the first time: it decides whether the 12-tester gate applies to you, and changing it later means a new account and migrating your apps.

  • Use an email you'll still control in five years — this account becomes the permanent owner of everything. Go accounts.google.com — create or sign in to the account you'll register with
    Why

    This account owns the membership, the apps, and the money. Transferring apps between accounts is possible but slow and conditional, and losing access to the underlying Google account can mean losing the developer account with it.

    Two practical warnings. First, don't use a personal account you share across your life — you'll be granting it to build tools and possibly teammates. Second, if you might ever hire or sell, an address like dev@yourcompany.com beats a personal Gmail, because the mailbox can outlive the person.

  • Not optional — Google requires it for Play Console access, and blocks sensitive actions without it. Guide support.google.com — Turn on 2-Step Verification
    Why

    Your developer account can push software to other people's phones. Google treats it as a high-value target and requires 2-Step Verification on accounts that can publish. You'll be confirming sign-ins fairly often, so make sure the second factor is a device you actually keep nearby.

  • Personal — fastest to register; identity verified with your ID. But: the 12-tester closed-test gate applies to every app, and if you monetize, your legal name and address are shown publicly.
    Organization — your company is the public developer, teammates get their own logins, and the 12-tester requirement does not apply. Requires a D-U-N-S Number and that you're authorized to act for the entity.     Compare Required information for a Play Console developer account
    Why

    Three things actually differ, and only the first is obvious.

    1. What's public. Personal accounts show your developer name on every listing — and once you sell anything, your legal name and physical address too. Organizations show the company's details instead.

    2. The testing gate. Personal accounts created after November 13, 2023 must pass the 12-testers-for-14-days closed test on each app before it can go to production. Organization accounts skip this entirely. If you have a registered business, this alone can save two weeks per app.

    3. It's not a switch. Moving from Personal to Organization means a new account and transferring each app across. If there's any real chance you'll want a company name on the store, start as an Organization even though it's slower to set up.

  • Free. Check whether your entity already has one before applying — most registered companies do. Google requires it for organization registration. Lookup / apply dnb.com/duns  ·  What Google needs account requirements
    Why

    A D-U-N-S Number is a nine-digit business identifier issued by Dun & Bradstreet, a credit-reporting company. Google uses it as third-party proof that your company legally exists, rather than taking your word for it.

    The catch that ruins timelines: the name and address you type into Google's registration must match the D&B record exactly. A different street abbreviation or a missing “LLC” will stall the registration, and fixing a D&B record is a separate process on their side. Look yours up first and copy the details verbatim.

  • Pay the one-time fee, pick your public developer name, and fill in the contact details Google will use to verify you. Register play.google.com/console/signup  ·  Help Register for a developer account
    Why

    One fee, unlimited apps, forever — there is no renewal. The developer name you pick here is what shows under your app on every listing, and while it can be edited later, it's identity: pick the name you want to be found by, not a throwaway.

    Registration also asks for a contact email and phone number that Google verifies immediately — use ones you can check right then, not a mailbox you look at monthly.

  • Government ID for personal accounts; registration documents and D-U-N-S match for organizations. Usually clears in hours to a few days — watch the email on the account. Where Play Console → the verification banner / task on the home screen  ·  What's checked Play Console requirements
    Why

    Google verifies every developer's identity before they can publish — it's how the store attaches a real person or entity to every app. Until it clears, you can poke around the Console but the publish path is blocked, so there's no point starting Phase 06 early. The usual snag is a name/document mismatch: the ID has to match the account's legal name exactly, the same way the D-U-N-S record does for organizations.

  • A completed registration shows the Play Console home with a “Create app” button and no outstanding verification banners. Check play.google.com/console — signed in with the developer account
    Why

    Worth pausing one minute on what the world will see: your developer name on every listing, your contact email, and — once you monetize on a personal account — your legal name and address. If any of that is a surprise, fix the account type now, in this phase, while nothing has shipped yet.

02
Agreements, tax & getting paid
One-time · revisit if your details change

This is the “getting paid” step. Shipping a free app with no purchases? Skip this whole phase — everything else in this manifest works without it. Just remember it exists, because the day you add a paid tier it becomes a multi-day blocker.

  • The profile that receives your payouts. It must be in the same legal name as the developer account — personal to personal, entity to entity. Guide Create a payments profile
    Why

    Google verifies that the payments profile holder matches the registered developer. A personal profile under a slightly different name than your registered entity is the usual reason this bounces — and each bounce is a support round-trip measured in days.

  • Go Play Console → Settings (gear) → Payments profile → link or create
    Why

    This is the answer to the most-asked money question in this whole process: “why can't I set a price / create an in-app product?” The controls aren't disabled with an explanation — they just aren't available until a payments profile is linked. Linking it makes them appear.

  • Bank account for payouts, plus the tax forms for your situation — US persons file a W-9-equivalent flow; non-US developers answer treaty questions that directly change how much withholding comes off US sales. Where pay.google.com → your payments profile → Settings, and the tax-info prompts inside Play Console's payments pages
    Why

    Google is legally required to withhold tax from payouts unless the right form says otherwise. Get it wrong and you keep less money — a non-US developer who skips the treaty section can lose a large slice of US revenue to withholding a correctly-filled form would have reduced. If you're not sure which form applies, that's an accountant question, not a developer question.

  • Opt in so your first $1M of revenue each year is charged 15% instead of 30%. Auto-renewing subscriptions are 15% regardless. Where Play Console → Settings → Developer account → “Service fee tier” (wording shifts; it's under account settings)  ·  Background service-fee changes · service fees
    Why

    The 15% rate on your first million is not automatic — it's an enrollment, and un-enrolled developers pay the full 30% on every sale. It costs nothing and there is no downside. This is the single highest-value five minutes in the entire money section.

  • A few business days. You can keep building and even run test tracks while it's pending — what you can't do is sell anything.
    Why

    Banking and tax verification run on Google's clock, not yours. Check back rather than waiting for an email — the status change isn't always announced, and a “pending” payments profile the week of launch is exactly the trap this phase exists to prevent.

Free app, no purchases, forever? You genuinely never need this phase. But remember the PREP warning: a free app can never become paid on Play — the only later path to revenue is in-app purchases, which is when this phase comes due.
03
Set up your computer
One-time · per machine

Android Studio is Google's app for building apps. You can technically build with nothing but Gradle and a JDK, but Studio bundles the SDK, the emulator, and the signing wizard — install it even if you plan to live in the terminal.

  • A multi-gigabyte download plus SDK components. Kick it off and do Phase 01 while it downloads. Install developer.android.com/studio — Windows, macOS, or Linux
    Why

    Studio ships everything as one install: the IDE, the Android SDK, a bundled JDK, and the emulator. Installing pieces separately is possible and is also how beginners end up with three JDKs and a build that works in one terminal and not another. One installer, one truth.

  • First launch downloads SDK platforms, build tools, and the emulator image. Let it finish before doing anything else.
    Why

    Until this completes, command-line builds fail with confusing errors about missing SDK packages or unaccepted licenses — errors that read like a broken project rather than an unfinished install.

  • From the project's android/ directory (once one exists), the Gradle wrapper should run and find the SDK. Accept any outstanding SDK licenses: cd android && ./gradlew --version
    ~/Library/Android/sdk/cmdline-tools/latest/bin/sdkmanager --licenses  # macOS default path; %LOCALAPPDATA%\Android\Sdk on Windows
    Why

    Anything that builds your app without opening Studio's window — an agent, a build script, CI — goes through the Gradle wrapper. The two classic failures are a missing ANDROID_HOME/SDK location and unaccepted licenses, both of which fail with walls of text that boil down to one unrun command. Verifying now means Phase 05 is boring, which is what you want Phase 05 to be.

  • In Studio, open the android/ folder itself — not the repo root, and not a file inside it. Wait for the Gradle sync to finish before judging any red squiggles. Typical path File → Open → select the android/ directory (Capacitor/RN/Flutter projects keep the native project there)
    Why

    Studio only understands Gradle projects. Open the repo root of a Capacitor or React Native app and you get a file browser with no build, no run button, and errors that look like a broken project. And the first Gradle sync downloads dependencies — judging errors before it finishes is judging a half-loaded page.

  • Enable Developer options (tap Build number seven times in Settings → About phone), turn on USB debugging, plug it in, and confirm adb devices lists it.
    Why

    The emulator covers most testing, but notifications, cameras, performance, and the actual install-from-Play flow behave differently on hardware. You'll also want a real device to walk through the tester opt-in flow yourself in Phase 06 before asking twelve other people to do it — debugging their confusion secondhand is far slower than hitting it firsthand.

  • Android Google Play Console app
    Why

    Release status changes, review verdicts, and new user reviews arrive as push notifications instead of you refreshing a browser tab. It can also halt a staged rollout from anywhere — genuinely useful the week something goes wrong.

04
Register the app & its identity
Per app

Everything here is scoped to one app, and two of these decisions are effectively permanent — the package name and the free/paid choice. You'll repeat this whole phase, quickly and with less agonising, for every new project.

  • Reverse-DNS format: com.yourname.appname. Lowercase letters, numbers and dots; each segment starts with a letter. Must be unique across every app Google has ever seen — and com.example.* is refused outright.
    Why

    The package name (applicationId in Gradle) is your app's true name as far as Android is concerned. It's how Play knows an update is an update rather than a new app, how purchases attach to your product, and how the OS decides which stored data belongs to you.

    You cannot change it after your first upload. Changing it means a brand-new app record and a brand-new Play listing — zero reviews, zero installs, and existing users never get another update. Treat it like naming a child, not naming a variable.

    Practical picks: own a domain? Reverse it — com.slapstax.slapstax. Don't? com.yourfullname.appname is perfectly acceptable and nobody will ever comment on it. Avoid anything containing “test”, “demo”, or a version number.

  • App name, default language, app or game, free or paid. The free/paid toggle is the permanent one: free can never become paid. Go Play Console → All apps → Create app  ·  Guide Create and set up your app
    Why

    This creates the app record — the container the listing, releases, and declarations all hang off. Nothing goes public yet; creating the record early is free and lets you fill in Phases 07–08 while the code is still cooking.

    The free/paid choice is asked casually here and is the least reversible thing on the page. If there's any chance you'll charge for the app itself (rather than via in-app purchases), pick paid now — you can always lower a price to zero later per country, but a free app is locked free forever.

  • Where android/app/build.gradle → defaultConfig → applicationId — and keep any framework config that mirrors it (capacitor.config.json's appId, app.json) agreeing
    Why

    Play matches uploads to the app record by this exact string. A typo here doesn't error locally — it builds fine and then uploads as a different, unknown app. Set it once, grep for the old value to catch every mirror, and never touch it again.

  • One command, two passwords you choose, one file that must never be committed to git and never exist in only one place: keytool -genkeypair -v -keystore upload-keystore.jks -alias upload -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 -validity 10000
    Why

    The keystore holds the private key that signs every build you upload. It's a secret in both directions: anyone who has it can push builds toward your users, and committing it to a public repo is a real, recurring incident pattern — which is why it lives outside git, with its passwords in a password manager and the file itself backed up somewhere that survives a dead laptop.

    The softener: with Play App Signing (next steps), this is only your upload key, and a lost one can be reset through Play Console support. That turns “lost keystore” from the app-ending catastrophe it was historically into a support ticket — but a leaked one is still a leaked signing key. Guard it accordingly.

  • A key.properties file (untracked) holding the paths and passwords, read by a signingConfigs block in build.gradle — or use Android Studio's wizard per-build instead: Build → Generate Signed App Bundle. Reference developer.android.com — sign your app  ·  add key.properties, *.jks to .gitignore
    Why

    The Gradle wiring is what lets ./gradlew bundleRelease produce a signed bundle unattended — which is what an agent, a script, or future-you at midnight actually runs. The Studio wizard works too and asks for the passwords each time; the properties-file route trades a one-time setup for never typing them again. Either way the passwords stay out of the repo.

  • Enrollment happens automatically with your first upload: Google generates and holds the app signing key (what devices actually verify), and your keystore from step 4 becomes the upload key (what proves an upload came from you). Read Use Play App Signing
    Why

    Every “signed with the wrong key” error for the rest of this app's life is this two-key system talking. The mental model: your upload key gets a bundle to Google; Google's app signing key gets it to devices. You never handle the app signing key, it can't be lost by you, and the upload key is resettable via support. Nothing to do in this step but read — five minutes here decodes an entire genre of future error message.

05
Build the release artifact
Every release

The loop you'll run for every version, forever: bump, build, upload. Play accepts one format for new apps — the Android App Bundle (.aab) — and the whole phase exists to produce one signed correctly.

  • npm run build && npx cap sync android  # Capacitor example — your stack's equivalent
    Why

    The native build packages whatever web build is sitting in the sync directory — it does not rebuild your web app for you. Skipping this ships last week's interface inside this week's release, and nothing errors: it's the single most common “my update didn't change anything” on hybrid stacks.

  • versionCode is an integer that must be strictly higher than every upload before it — even failed ones. versionName is the human-readable version users see. Where android/app/build.gradle → defaultConfig  ·  Reference version your app
    Why

    Play tracks builds by versionCode alone and refuses any upload that reuses one — “Version code 2 has already been used” is the toll for forgetting. A boring habit that prevents it entirely: bump the number as the first edit of any release day, not the last.

  • Either route produces the same artifact: Studio Build → Generate Signed App Bundle / APK… → Android App Bundle → pick your keystore → release
    Terminal cd android && ./gradlew bundleRelease
    Why

    The AAB is not an installable app — it's a publishing format. You upload the bundle, and Play generates optimized APKs per device from it (right screen densities, right architectures), which is why it's required: it's what makes installs small. If Gradle finishes but the bundle is unsigned, the signing config from Phase 04 isn't being read — fix that rather than signing by hand.

  • Path android/app/build/outputs/bundle/release/app-release.aab — confirm the file is fresh (check the timestamp) and not tiny
    Why

    Gradle happily leaves old artifacts sitting in outputs/. Uploading a stale bundle from a previous build is silent — same filename, wrong contents — and the timestamp check is the two-second habit that catches it.

  • Install the release build on a device or emulator and open every main screen once. bundletool can install straight from the .aab; or build assembleRelease and adb install the APK.
    Why

    Release builds differ from debug builds in ways that only show at runtime — minification can strip something reflection needed, a debug-only flag can gate real content, a misconfigured base URL can point at localhost. Thirty seconds of tapping through the release build catches the class of bug that otherwise becomes a one-star review titled “blank screen”.

06
Test before you release
Every release · strongly recommended

Play has three testing tracks before production: internal (instant, up to 100 people), closed (invited lists), and open (anyone with the link). For a personal account created after Nov 13, 2023, the closed track isn't optional — it's the mandatory 14-day gate in front of production. Start this phase the moment you have any build at all.

  • Fastest loop: available to your listed testers within minutes, no review. Use it for your own devices and your first honest look at the app installed from Play. Go Play Console → your app → Testing → Internal testing → Create new release → upload the .aab  ·  Guide Set up an open, closed, or internal test
    Why

    Internal testing is where upload problems surface cheaply — signing errors, version-code collisions, a broken first-run — while the audience is just you. It also triggers your first pre-launch report (step 6) for free. Everything downstream goes smoother when the first upload's mistakes happened here.

  • The track whose test satisfies the production-access requirement. Promote the internal build to it, or upload fresh. Go Play Console → your app → Testing → Closed testing → create/manage track → Create new release
    Why

    Only closed testing counts toward the 12-tester requirement — internal and open testing don't. If the gate applies to your account, this track is the one whose clock matters, so create it early and treat it as the app's real home until production opens.

  • Add tester emails (or a Google Group) to the track, then copy the opt-in URL from the track's Testers tab. Each tester must open that link, accept, and install the app — being on the list alone counts for nothing. Where Closed testing track → Testers tab → email lists + “Copy link” under How testers join your test
    Why

    The #1 confusion in all of Play testing: adding someone's email does not make them a tester. The email list is an allow-list; the opt-in link is the actual door. Send the link with two sentences of instructions (“open on your Android phone, tap Accept, then Install”), and expect to re-send it — a third of any tester group loses the first message.

  • The requirement is 12 testers opted in continuously for the 14 days before you apply — recruit 15–18 so dropouts don't reset your clock. Friends, family, coworkers all count; they just need Android phones and Google accounts. The rule App testing requirements for new personal accounts  ·  Google's own guide Everything about the 12-testers requirement
    Why

    This is the two-week clock from PREP, now live. “Continuously” is the sharp edge: a tester who opts out on day 10 can drop you below 12 and stall the count, which is why you recruit a buffer. Keep testers mildly engaged — a couple of updates during the window is fine and looks like real testing, because it is. Google also asks about your test when you apply for production access, so genuine usage now writes those answers for you.

    Organization account? This step doesn't apply — tick it and move on.

  • Watch the track's crash reports and pre-launch results, ask testers what confused them, ship fixes to the same track. Each upload needs a bumped versionCode (Phase 05 loop).
    Why

    Twelve real users on real devices will surface what the emulator never did: a keyboard covering an input, a notification that never fired, a screen unreadable in dark mode. The 14 days are mandatory anyway — spending them fixing real feedback converts a compliance wait into an actually better launch build.

  • Automatic on track uploads: Google's robo-crawler opens the app on a fleet of real devices and reports crashes, layout issues, accessibility flags, and security warnings. Go Play Console → your app → Test and release → Pre-launch report  ·  About pre-launch reports
    Why

    It's a free device farm. A crash that appears on three cheap devices in the report is a crash a real user hits on launch day — and the same automated checks inform review, so a clean report is also cheap insurance against a “broken app” rejection you'd otherwise discover a week later.

  • When the 14 days complete, the Console's dashboard offers the application — short questions about your test, your testers, and readiness. Answer from what actually happened; approval typically takes a few days. Where Play Console → your app → Dashboard → the “Apply for production” task appears when the requirement is met
    Why

    A human-reviewed application, not a rubber stamp — vague or copy-pasted answers get rejected and cost you another cycle. Concrete beats polished: what your testers did, what you fixed because of them, why the app is ready. Once granted, production access is permanent for this app and Phase 10's rollout unlocks.

Open testing (anyone with the link, optional cap) exists too — useful later for a public beta with thousands of users, and it can run alongside production forever. It doesn't count toward the 12-tester requirement, so it's an afterthought on a first launch, not a step.
07
Store listing content
Per appUpdated per release

Everything the public sees, all on one Console page. Play has no separate keyword field — the name and descriptions are your search presence, which makes this copy do double duty. Listing edits don't require a new build, but they do go through review.

  • Go Play Console → your app → Grow → Store presence → Main store listing
    Why

    Shown everywhere the app appears. Stuffing it with keywords (“Sleep Tracker - Alarm, Sounds, Calm”) is an explicit metadata-policy violation on Play, not a growth hack — the name should be the name, and the descriptions carry the search terms.

  • The one line under your name in search results and at the top of the listing. Say what the app does, plainly — it's the highest-leverage sentence you'll write.
    Why

    Most people who see your listing read exactly this much before deciding. It's also indexed for search. A vague tagline (“Your journey starts here”) spends both jobs on nothing; “Daily scripture with guided study, journal, and audio” spends them on installs.

  • Lead with what the app does in one sentence, then short scannable sections. Paste from the drafted store-assets/play-listing.md rather than composing in the form.
    Why

    Indexed for search and read by the minority who tap “read more” — so the first two lines matter most, and natural mentions of the terms people would search for do real work. Repeating a keyword unnaturally is the same metadata violation as stuffing the title; write for humans and the index follows.

  • 32-bit PNG, up to 1MB. This is the store-listing icon — separate from the launcher icons inside the app, and it should match them. Specs preview assets — specifications
    Why

    Google masks the corners itself, so upload the full square. A listing icon that doesn't match the installed launcher icon reads as sloppy at best and impersonation at worst — same art, both places.

  • The wide banner from Phase 00 (or make one now): app mark + name on a brand background, JPEG or 24-bit PNG.
    Why

    The form blocks publishing without it, which is how most people discover it exists. It tops your listing, backs any promo video, and is what editorial placements use — the one asset with no iOS equivalent and therefore the one nobody has ready.

  • JPEG or 24-bit PNG (no alpha), each side 320–3840px, portrait 9:16 for the phone slot. First two or three are what most people actually see — order them as a story.
    Why

    Screenshots are the listing's real sales pitch. Play's looser size rules make automation easy (Phase 00 shoots them headlessly), and re-shooting the set when the UI changes should cost seconds, not an evening of hand-cropping. The alpha-channel refusal is silent — flatten PNGs before upload.

  • Separate 7-inch and 10-inch sets qualify the listing as tablet-optimized. Skip freely if phones are the audience.
    Why

    Without them, tablet users see your phone shots and Play may rank the app lower in tablet search. With them, you're promising the layout actually works on tablets — upload them only if that's true, because the mismatch generates the reviews.

  • Pick the category that matches what the app does; add the contact email (required, shown publicly), and website/phone if you have them. Go Play Console → your app → Grow → Store presence → Store settings
    Why

    Category drives which charts and browse shelves you can appear on — miscategorizing into a “less crowded” one is a metadata violation, not a strategy. The contact email is where users and Google both reach you; a monitored address here prevents the “developer unreachable” class of problem.

  • Put the drafted privacy-policy.html somewhere public and stable — GitHub Pages off the app's repo is the fastest zero-cost host. The URL itself is entered in Phase 08.
    Why

    Required for every app, offline ones included, and the policy must also be reachable from inside the app. A dead link, a “coming soon” page, or a policy describing a different product is a rejection — and Google re-checks it after launch, so the URL needs to stay alive, not just survive review day.

08
Privacy & compliance
Per app

Play Console gathers every declaration on one page — App content — and won't release anything while an item sits incomplete. Google asks a lot up front; every answer here is checked against what the app actually does, some of them after launch.

  • Go Play Console → your app → Monitor and improve → Policy → App content (the Console surfaces it as tasks on the Dashboard too)
    Why

    Every declaration below lives here, each with its own Start/Manage button and status. The page is the honest to-do list — when a release won't roll out and nothing says why, an incomplete item on this page is the answer more often than anything else in the Console.

  • The URL hosted in Phase 07, step 9. Must load, must describe this app, must match the Data safety answers.
    Why

    This link renders on your public listing, and reviewers open it. It's also cross-checked against the Data safety form — a policy that says “we collect nothing” against a form that declares analytics is a contradiction with your name on it.

  • Does the app show ads? One toggle — and it must agree with the content rating answers, the Data safety form, and reality. About ads declaration
    Why

    “Contains ads” shows as a label on your listing. Declaring no while an ad SDK sits in the dependency tree is detectable — Google scans the binary — and is a straightforward policy strike. If a library bundles ad code you don't use, that's exactly the kind of finding docs/STORE-FACTS.md exists to surface before this toggle does.

  • Honest questions about violence, sexuality, gambling, language, user-generated content. One pass generates official ratings for every region at once. Requirements content rating requirements  ·  The system IARC
    Why

    Unrated apps get removed, and deliberately under-rating to dodge an age band is enforced against the account, not just the app. Over-rating costs too — it walls off audiences and stores. Answer straight; the questionnaire takes ten minutes and only hurts when gamed.

  • Declare the age groups the app is for. Include anyone under 13 and the Families policies apply — stricter ads, data, and content rules. Guide target audience and content settings
    Why

    This is a legal declaration (children's-privacy law territory), not a marketing choice. The trap runs both directions: declaring “including children” without meeting Families requirements gets rejected, while an app that clearly appeals to kids but declares adults-only gets flagged for the mismatch. Most general-audience apps correctly declare 13+ or 18+ and move on.

  • Data type by data type: collected? shared? encrypted in transit? deletable? Fill it from docs/STORE-FACTS.md — the grepped facts — not from what the app is “supposed to” do. Local-only storage is not “collected” in Play's definition. Guide Data safety section — how to complete it
    Why

    The answers render as the privacy label on your listing, and Google verifies them against the app's observed behavior — including every SDK you link, whose collection is your declaration to make. A form that under-declares is a policy violation that can remove the app after launch, which is a worse day than a rejection. The offline-app fast path is real: no data leaves the device → a short form, honestly answered, done in minutes.

  • Two required paths: a discoverable in-app way to delete the account, and a web URL (entered in the Data safety form) where users can request deletion without reinstalling. The rule account deletion requirements
    Why

    Stricter than Apple's version: the web link is mandatory precisely because people delete apps before accounts. The link shows publicly on your listing's Data safety section. No accounts in the app? Answer “no account creation” in the form and this whole item collapses to one radio button.

  • If any part of the app sits behind a login or paywall, provide working test credentials and steps. Fully open app? Declare exactly that. Where App content → App access  ·  Guide prepare your app for review
    Why

    Reviewers who can't get past a login review what they can see: a login wall. Credentials that are real, current, and re-verified before every submission are the cheapest rejection insurance that exists — an expired test password is the classic self-inflicted delay.

  • A run of mostly-“no” questions for a typical app: is this a news app, does it target government audiences, does it provide financial or health features. Two minutes if none apply; real forms if one does.
    Why

    Each is a regulated category with its own verification (news publishers prove identity, financial apps document licensing, health apps face data rules). The catch for everyone else is scope creep by accident — a devotional app with a “wellness” blurb isn't a health app, and answering as if it were buys verification work nothing required. Answer for what the app does, not what its marketing gestures at.

  • To distribute in the EU you declare whether you're a “trader” (earning from apps professionally) and verify contact details, which are then shown publicly on EU listings. Where Play Console → Settings → Developer account → the DSA / trader-status section  ·  Background conditions for Google Play in the EEA
    Why

    EU consumer law, not Google preference: apps from undeclared developers stop being distributable in the EU, and monetizing as a declared trader means a verified address and contact shown to EU users. If that's unacceptable as an individual, the honest alternatives are a business entity or excluding EU countries in Phase 09 — silence just removes the app there for you.

The through-line: the Data safety form, the privacy policy, the account-deletion answer, and the actual binary must all tell the same story. That's why Phase 00 greps the code and writes docs/STORE-FACTS.md first — every answer on this page comes from one file, so they can't drift apart.
09
Pricing & availability
Per app

Where the app is available and what it costs. Everything here is freely editable later — except the one switch you already know about.

  • Set in Phase 04 when the app record was created; this is the final check before anything publishes. Free is permanent. Paid can drop to any lower price, including effectively free per-country sales, but a free app can never charge for itself.
    Why

    Worth its own box because this page is the last place the decision is cheap. After first publish, the only revenue paths for a free app are in-app purchases and subscriptions (Phase 12) — fine for most modern apps, fatal if your plan was a $4.99 up-front price you forgot to set.

  • Per-track country lists. Defaulting to “all countries” is fine for a free app with no legal exposure; EU availability requires the Phase 08 trader declaration. Go Play Console → your app → Release → Production → Countries / regions
    Why

    Broad-by-default is the Play norm (the opposite of the cautious-iOS instinct) because there's no per-country listing work unless you localize. The reasons to exclude a country are real ones: legal/regulatory exposure, content laws, or the EU declaration you've decided not to make. You can add or remove countries any time; removals don't uninstall the app for existing users.

  • Set a base price; Google converts per country with sensible local price points, all overridable. Requires the Phase 02 payments profile.
    Why

    Local price points matter more than the exchange rate — markets have strong customary tiers, and the auto-suggested points land on them. Override only with a reason. Price changes are free and instant forever; it's only the free/paid identity that's locked.

  • The Phase 02 enrollment is account-level — thirty seconds to verify it's active before revenue starts flowing at the wrong rate.
    Why

    Un-enrolled means 30% off the top of every sale that should have been 15%. There's no retroactive refund for fees paid before enrollment — the check costs nothing, the miss costs double.

  • Opens the listing before launch so users can sign up for an install-on-release notification. Skip for a first app unless there's a real audience waiting.
    Why

    It's a marketing tool with a deadline attached — pre-registration commits you to launching within a window. With an existing audience it converts hype into day-one installs; without one it's a countdown clock ticking at nobody. YAGNI for launch number one.

10
Submit for review
Every release

On Play, “submitting” is rolling out a production release — review happens on the way. Personal accounts need Phase 06's production access granted first; everyone needs Phase 08 complete, because incomplete declarations hard-block the rollout button.

  • The Console's Dashboard and Publishing overview list exactly what's missing — declarations, listing assets, country selection. Work the list to zero before creating the release. Go Play Console → your app → Dashboard — expand the remaining setup tasks
    Why

    Unlike Apple's submit-then-discover flow, Play tells you up front — but only on this page. The classic frustration is hunting a greyed-out “rollout” button's reason in release settings when the answer was a red item on the Dashboard the whole time.

  • Off (default): the release goes live automatically the moment review approves it. On: approved changes wait until you press publish. Where Play Console → your app → Publishing overview → Managed publishing  ·  Guide control when changes are published
    Why

    Review finishing at 3am and your app launching while you sleep is the default behavior. Managed publishing decouples “approved” from “live” — the tool for coordinating with an announcement, a website, or an iOS release going out the same day. The trade: with it on, nothing publishes until you press the button, including the fix you forgot was queued behind it.

  • Promote the tested build from its track (or upload the .aab fresh), write the release notes, review the summary, and confirm rollout. Go Play Console → your app → Release → Production → Create new release → promote from Closed testing  ·  Guide prepare and roll out a release
    Why

    Promoting the exact build your testers ran is the point of the tracks system — same file, same behavior, no “works in beta, broken in prod” surprise from a hasty rebuild. The release notes field (500 characters) shows on the listing; “bug fixes and improvements” is a wasted line on launch day.

  • First apps from new accounts sit at the long end; updates from established accounts often clear in hours. The status lives on the Publishing overview, and verdicts arrive by email and Console inbox.
    Why

    Review time is not a signal — a long wait doesn't mean trouble, and nothing you resubmit makes it faster (resubmitting restarts the clock). Plan launches with a week of slack and the wait is boring instead of terrifying. Publish your app covers what each status means.

  • The rejection email and the Console's Policy status page name the specific policy and, usually, the specific screen or declaration. Fix that; don't shotgun changes. Go Play Console → your app → Monitor and improve → Policy → Policy status
    Why

    Most rejections are narrow: a Data safety mismatch, a broken privacy-policy link, a screenshot showing content the app doesn't have. The named policy is the whole assignment — fix precisely that, resubmit, done. Blind-fixing five other things adds new surface area to review and turns one cycle into three. Genuinely wrong verdict? There's an appeal path from the same page; polite and specific beats outraged.

  • With managed publishing on, press the publish button now. Then find the listing on a real device: search for it, install it, open it once. Your public URL https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=<your.package.name>
    Why

    “Approved” and “findable” are different moments — listing propagation across regions takes from minutes to a day or two. The install-it-yourself ritual catches the last-mile embarrassments (a listing typo, a wrong screenshot, a first-run crash on a clean device) while the audience is still one person.

Release & monitor
Every release

Launch is a beginning, not a finish line. Play is unusually mechanical about post-launch quality — crash and ANR rates directly change your visibility — so the monitoring here is leverage, not hygiene.

  • Release to a percentage of users (say 10–20%), watch vitals, then expand to 100%. First-time releases go to everyone; the staging option appears from your first update on. Guide staged rollouts
    Why

    A bug that reaches 10% of users is an incident; the same bug at 100% is your rating for the next six months. Staging costs one extra click and a day of patience — the cheapest insurance in the whole release loop, and the habit to build on update number one.

  • A staged rollout can be halted instantly — no one new gets the bad build while you fix and roll a new version. Find the button before you need it. Where Play Console → your app → Release → Production → the active release's Halt rollout (also in the Play Console phone app)
    Why

    Halting is damage control, not rollback — users who already updated keep the build, but the bleeding stops. The reason to locate it in advance: you'll want it during the worst hour of your launch week, on your phone, not after a frantic search of an unfamiliar menu.

  • Crash rate and ANR (“app not responding”) rate, against Google's bad-behavior thresholds — currently around 1.1% for user-perceived crashes and 0.5% for ANRs. Exceeding them suppresses your visibility. Go Play Console → your app → Monitor and improve → Android vitals  ·  Guide Android vitals
    Why

    This is the mechanical part: Play demotes apps over the thresholds in search and browse, and can put a warning on the listing itself. It's also your free crash reporter — stack traces by device and OS version with no SDK added. The first 48 hours after any release is when a regression shows here first, before the reviews arrive to tell you about it less politely.

  • Reply to bug reports, confusion, and fixable complaints — replies are public, and reviewers get notified and can update their rating. Go Play Console → your app → Monitor and improve → Ratings and reviews → Reviews
    Why

    A one-star “crashes on open” answered with “fixed in today's update — would you give it another try?” becomes a four-star more often than seems plausible. Every reply is also read by future browsers deciding whether the developer is alive. Skip the thank-you replies to five-star reviews; answer the fixable ones.

  • Link https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=<your.package.name>  ·  Badge “Get it on Google Play” generator
    Why

    The badge generator produces the licensed artwork in every language — using it keeps you inside Google's brand rules, and the recognized badge converts better on a website than any hand-made button. This is the link for your site, your email signature, and the “it's live” post.

  • Every August 31, Play's target-API floor rises. Existing apps that fall too far behind stop being visible to new users on newer devices. Set a yearly reminder for June to check and bump. The requirement target API level requirements
    Why

    The launch checklist item that outlives the launch: a finished, stable app that nobody touches for two years quietly vanishes from search for most users — not removed, just hidden — and the fix is usually one number in build.gradle, a test pass, and a routine update. The June reminder turns an emergency into an afternoon.

Monetization
Per app · only if you're charging

Selling digital things inside the app — premium unlocks, subscriptions, coins. Prerequisite: the Phase 02 payments profile. The governing rule: digital goods go through Google Play's billing system, full stop.

  • Anything consumed in the app (features, content, subscriptions) must use Google Play's billing system. Physical goods and real-world services are exempt and use whatever processor you like. Docs Play Billing overview
    Why

    Routing around it — a credit-card form for a premium unlock, a “pay on our website” link — is a removal-grade violation, same as iOS. The service fee is the price of the rails. (Regional exceptions for alternative billing exist in a few jurisdictions; they're paperwork-heavy and nothing a first app should design around.)

  • One-time products and subscriptions, each with an ID, name, and price. Product IDs are permanent once used — name them like the identifiers they are (premium_monthly, not test1). Go Play Console → your app → Monetize → Products → In-app products / Subscriptions
    Why

    The app queries these by ID at runtime — code and Console must agree on the exact strings, and a shipped ID is forever (users own purchases against it). Subscriptions carry the design decisions: base plans, offers, trials, and grace periods all live on the product, not in your code.

  • The Play Billing Library natively, or the wrapper your stack uses (Capacitor/RN plugins, RevenueCat, etc.). Purchases must be acknowledged after grant, or Play automatically refunds them.
    Why

    The acknowledgment rule is the classic first-integration bug: everything works, money arrives, and three days later every purchase silently refunds because the app never acknowledged them. Whatever library you use, confirm it acknowledges — and that entitlement is checked on restore/reinstall, because “I paid and lost my premium” is the angriest review there is.

  • Gmail addresses listed account-wide as license testers can make test purchases with no real charge, with shortened subscription periods for testing renewals. Go Play Console (account level) → Settings → License testing  ·  Guide test with application licensing
    Why

    Real product IDs, real purchase flow, fake money — the only honest way to test the whole pipeline including renewal and cancellation. Subscription periods compress (a month becomes minutes), so a full subscribe→renew→cancel→expire cycle is an afternoon instead of two months of waiting.

  • As a license tester on a real device: buy, confirm the unlock, reinstall and confirm restore, cancel and confirm graceful expiry.
    Why

    Each leg fails independently and only shows on device: the buy can work while restore doesn't (entitlement checked only at purchase), and expiry can lock users out mid-grace-period. Fifteen minutes of walking the loop is the difference between shipping a store and shipping a support queue.

  • Price, billing period, trial terms, and how to cancel — visible on the paywall itself, with Terms and Privacy links. Then keep the Data safety form consistent with any purchase data collected.
    Why

    Subscription-transparency rules are enforced at review and by consumer law: a paywall that hides the renewal price behind a tap, or buries “cancel anytime” is a rejection on its own. The boring paywall that states the price twice is the one that passes — and converts fine anyway.

Glossary
Reference

Google's screens and error messages assume you already know these words. Nothing else in this file requires reading it in order — come back when a term bites.

Play Console
The website where your apps, listings, releases, testers, declarations and money live. play.google.com/console.
Developer account
Your $25-once Play Console registration — personal or organization. The permanent owner of every app you publish.
Package name / applicationId
Your app's permanent true name, in reverse-DNS form: com.yourname.appname. Cannot be changed after first upload; com.example.* is refused.
AAB / Android App Bundle
The .aab publishing format Play requires. Not installable itself — Play generates optimized per-device APKs from it.
APK
The installable app file format. What devices actually run; what Play builds from your bundle. You only handle one directly for local testing.
Gradle
Android's build system. ./gradlew is the per-project wrapper script; build.gradle holds the app's identity and version facts.
Keystore
A password-protected file (.jks) holding signing keys. Never committed to git, always backed up in more than one place.
Upload key
The key you hold and sign uploads with. Proves a bundle came from you. Lost ones can be reset through Play Console support.
App signing key
The key Google holds under Play App Signing — the one devices actually verify. You never touch it and can't lose it.
Play App Signing
The default two-key system: you sign with the upload key, Google re-signs for delivery with the app signing key. The source of every “wrong key” error message.
versionCode vs versionName
versionCode is an internal integer that must strictly increase on every upload. versionName (1.2.0) is what users see.
Target API level / targetSdkVersion
The Android version your app declares it's built for. Play enforces a floor that rises every August 31; too old = upload refused, or the app hidden from newer devices.
Track
A release channel: internal testing, closed testing, open testing, or production. Builds promote between tracks without rebuilding.
Internal testing
Instant distribution to up to 100 listed testers, no review. Your fastest loop.
Closed testing
Invite-only track (email lists or Google Groups). The only track that counts toward the 12-tester production-access requirement.
Open testing
Public beta — anyone with the link can join. Can run alongside production.
Opt-in link
The URL a tester must personally accept before Play will show them the test build. Being on the email list alone does nothing.
Production access
Permission for a new personal account's app to release publicly — granted on application after the 12-testers-for-14-days closed test.
Pre-launch report
Google's automatic robo-test of each track upload on a fleet of real devices: crashes, layout, accessibility, security findings.
Data safety
The form declaring what data the app collects and shares, rendered as the privacy section on your listing. Verified against actual app behavior.
Content rating / IARC
The questionnaire that generates official age ratings for every region at once. Unrated apps get removed.
Managed publishing
A switch that holds approved changes until you explicitly press publish — decoupling “passed review” from “went live”.
Staged rollout
Releasing an update to a percentage of users, expandable or haltable. Available from your first update on.
Android vitals
Play's built-in quality telemetry — crash rate, ANR rate, battery misbehavior — with thresholds that affect your store visibility when exceeded.
ANR
“Application Not Responding” — the app's UI thread froze long enough that Android offered the user a kill button. Counts against vitals like a crash.
Play Billing
Google's in-app purchase system. Anything selling digital goods inside an Android app on Play must use it.
License tester
A Gmail address allowed to make test purchases with no real charge and compressed subscription periods.
Policy status
The Console page listing every policy finding against your app — where rejection details and appeal links live.
Trader status (DSA)
An EU Digital Services Act declaration with publicly-shown, verified contact details. Required to distribute in the EU.
D-U-N-S Number
A nine-digit business identifier from Dun & Bradstreet — Google's third-party proof that an organization legally exists.

Build & upload errors

What you seeWhat it means & what to do
“Version code X has already been used”You reused a versionCode — Play counts every past upload, failed ones included. Bump it in build.gradle, rebuild, re-upload. Phase 05, step 2.
“Your Android App Bundle is signed with the wrong key”The bundle wasn't signed with this app's registered upload key — usually a second keystore lying around, or a fresh one after a laptop change. Sign with the original, or request an upload-key reset via Play App Signing support.
“You uploaded a debuggable APK or Android App Bundle”The release build has debuggable true — you probably uploaded a debug build by mistake. Build bundleRelease and check the output path.
“You need to use a different package name” on com.example.*Play refuses the scaffold default outright. Set a real applicationId — Phase 04, step 1.
Upload refused for target API levelThe bundle targets below Play's current floor. Raise targetSdkVersion, fix what the runtime changes break, re-upload. See the current requirement.
SDK location not foundGradle can't find the Android SDK — missing local.properties/ANDROID_HOME on this machine. Open the project once in Android Studio (it writes the file), or set the env var. Phase 03, step 3.
Failed to install the following Android SDK packages … licences have not been acceptedRun sdkmanager --licenses and accept. Phase 03, step 3.
Gradle sync fails the moment the project opensUsually a JDK/Gradle version mismatch or half-downloaded dependencies. Let the first sync finish on a good connection; use Studio's embedded JDK before debugging exotic causes.
Release build crashes where debug didn'tMinification (R8) stripped something reflection needed, or a debug-only flag gated real behavior. Check the pre-launch report's stack trace; add keep rules or fix the flag. Phase 05, step 5 exists for exactly this.
App installs but shows last week's interfaceWeb assets were never rebuilt/synced before the native build. Re-run the build+sync step — Phase 05, step 1.

Play Console confusion

What you seeWhat it means & what to do
Can't create a production release at allPersonal account without production access yet — the 12-tester closed test hasn't been run/passed. Phase 06. There is no way to skip it.
Rollout button greyed out, no clear reasonAn incomplete App content declaration or missing listing asset. The Dashboard's task list names it — Phase 10, step 1.
Testers say they can't find or install the test buildThey never opened the opt-in link (the email list alone does nothing), or they're signed into a different Google account on the phone. Re-send the link with instructions — Phase 06, step 3.
“Why can't I set a price / create in-app products?”No payments profile linked. Phase 02.
Approved days ago, still not liveManaged publishing is on and holding it — press publish. Phase 10, step 2. (Or it's live and still propagating regionally; check the direct listing URL.)
Want to switch a free app to paidImpossible, permanently. The revenue path is in-app purchases (Phase 12) — or a new app record with a new package name and zero installs.
App vanished from the EU storeTrader status wasn't declared/verified. Phase 08, step 10.
Screenshot or icon upload silently refusedAlpha channel in a PNG, or dimensions outside spec. Flatten and check against the asset specs.
Every purchase refunds itself after ~3 daysPurchases aren't being acknowledged. Fix the billing integration — Phase 12, step 3.

Common rejections & removals, by policy

Policy areaWhy it happened
Data safety — inaccurate declarationThe form says one thing, the binary does another — an SDK collecting what you didn't declare is the classic. Re-grep, fix the form, resubmit. Can strike after launch.
User Data — privacy policyLink dead, page generic, or policy contradicts the Data safety form. Must be live, specific to this app, and linked both in Console and in-app.
User Data — account deletionAccounts can be created but not deleted in-app, or the required web deletion link is missing/broken.
Spam & Minimum FunctionalityThe app is a repackaged website or too thin to justify being an app — the most common death sentence for a first web-shell app. It needs to do something a bookmark can't (offline, notifications, native integration).
MetadataKeyword-stuffed title, screenshots showing features that don't exist, or competitor names in your listing text.
PermissionsA restricted permission (SMS, call log, all-files access, background location, accessibility) without an approved declaration and a core-functionality justification.
FamiliesThe target-audience declaration includes children but the app doesn't meet Families ads/data requirements — or clearly appeals to kids while declaring it doesn't.
PaymentsDigital goods sold outside Play Billing — a card form or external payment link for in-app content.
Subscriptions — transparencyRenewal price, period, or cancellation not disclosed on the paywall before purchase.
Intellectual propertyA trademark, brand, or content you don't have rights to — in the app, the name, the icon, or the screenshots.
Deceptive behaviorThe app does something the listing doesn't say, or claims functionality it doesn't have.
